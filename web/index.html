Why January, and not December

The category is violently seasonal; attention is not

The book and hobby category peaks in December at — and collapses to — in January. Franchise attention barely moves all year: it spans only — index points.

That gap is the whole argument. December's peak is made by the gift calendar, not by any surge of interest in the franchise. January is the month when shelves are cleared while attention does not fall, and it is the month the premiere pushes attention sharply up.

What that does to demand

The dashed line is the category with no premiere: a December spike, then a collapse. The bars are the three scenarios. They stay elevated through January, February and March, which is exactly when the category normally has nothing left to sell.

I impose the premiere response on the forecast rather than fitting it. No model can learn the effect of an event that has never happened in this series, so my multiples come from measured franchise events. These are three readings of that evidence, not confidence intervals.

The number to argue with first

The largest uncertainty in this work is not statistical. It is the assumption about how much of a rise in franchise attention shows up as a rise in category sales, set at —.

It is an assumption because I could not measure it. The elasticity of Canadian category sales to franchise attention came back at 0.05 with a confidence interval spanning zero. That is not evidence of no effect: the published category contains every bookshop and hobby shop in Canada, so one franchise inside it sits below the noise floor.
